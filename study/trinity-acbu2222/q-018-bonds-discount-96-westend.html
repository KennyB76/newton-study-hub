Q-018 — Bonds at Discount: West End Corporation

Problem (Section 2 — BONDS at 96):
On January 1, 2024, West End Corporation issued $500,000, 5%, 5-year bonds dated January 1, 2024, at 96. The bonds pay semiannual interest on January 1st and July 1st.

Why "at 96"?
"At 96" means bonds sold at 96% of face value. Investors paid only $480,000 for a $500,000 bond because the stated rate (5%) is below the market rate (~6%). Lower rate = lower price = discount.

1. Issue Date Journal Entry Both Methods

The issue entry is the same regardless of amortization method chosen.

1Cash received = $500,000 × 0.96 = $480,000

2Discount = Face − Cash = $500,000 − $480,000 = $20,000

3Record: debit Cash, debit Discount (contra-liability), credit Bonds Payable

January 1, 2024 — ISSUE DATE Cash 480,000 Discount on Bonds Payable 20,000 Bonds Payable 500,000 (Issued $500,000 face, 5%, 5-yr bonds at 96 = $480,000 proceeds)
Balance Sheet effect after issue:
Bonds Payable (face) $500,000
Less: Discount on Bonds ($20,000)
Carrying Value (book value) = $480,000

2. Interest Entry — Straight-Line Method SL

How Straight-Line Works

Spread the total discount evenly across all 10 periods. Simple — same amount every period.

SL Discount Amortization per period = Total Discount ÷ Total Periods = $20,000 ÷ 10 = $2,000 per period
Interest Expense (SL) = Cash Interest + Discount Amortization = $12,500 + $2,000 = $14,500 per period

This $14,500 is the same every single period under SL.

July 1, 2024 — FIRST INTEREST PAYMENT (Straight-Line) Interest Expense 14,500 Discount on Bonds Payable 2,000 Cash 12,500 (Cash interest paid + discount amortized equally over 10 periods)
January 1, 2025 — SECOND INTEREST PAYMENT (Straight-Line) Interest Expense 14,500 Discount on Bonds Payable 2,000 Cash 12,500 (Same entry repeats every period — that's the beauty of SL)

3. Interest Entry — Effective Interest Method EIM

How EIM Works

Interest expense = Carrying Value × Market Rate. As carrying value increases (discount amortizes), interest expense grows each period.

Interest Expense (EIM) = Carrying Value (beginning of period) × Market Rate = CV × 3% (semiannual) Discount Amortized = Interest Expense − Cash Interest = (CV × 3%) − $12,500 New Carrying Value = Old CV + Discount Amortized
July 1, 2024 — FIRST INTEREST PAYMENT (Effective Interest Method) Interest Expense = $480,000 × 3% = $14,400 Interest Expense 14,400 Discount on Bonds Payable 1,900 Cash 12,500 (EIM: expense based on carrying value × market rate)
January 1, 2025 — SECOND INTEREST PAYMENT (Effective Interest Method) New CV = $480,000 + $1,900 = $481,900 Interest Expense = $481,900 × 3% = $14,457 Interest Expense 14,457 Discount on Bonds Payable 1,957 Cash 12,500 (CV increased → interest expense slightly higher than period 1)

Rule to Remember:
Discount bond → CV starts LOW → CV rises to face (like climbing a ladder)
Premium bond → CV starts HIGH → CV falls to face (like descending stairs)
Both arrive at exactly $500,000 on maturity date.

Exam Tip: If the problem gives you a market rate → use EIM. If no market rate given → use SL. Both methods fully amortize the $20,000 discount by maturity. The journal entry structure is identical — only the dollar amounts differ.

8. Common Mistakes (5)

Mistake #1: Forgetting to debit Discount on Bonds Payable at issuance
Students often write: Cash 480,000 / Bonds Payable 480,000.
WRONG — Bonds Payable must always equal face value ($500,000). The $20,000 gap goes to Discount on Bonds Payable (debit — contra liability).
Mistake #2: Treating Discount amortization as a credit to Cash
Discount amortization is NOT a cash payment. It's a credit to Discount on Bonds Payable (which reduces the contra account, increasing CV). Cash only moves for the stated coupon payment ($12,500).
Mistake #3: Using the wrong rate in EIM
In EIM, use the market rate at issuance (3% semiannual), not the stated coupon rate (2.5%). The coupon rate determines cash, the market rate determines expense.
Mistake #4: Applying EIM amortization to the wrong base
EIM uses the beginning carrying value for each period, not the face value and not the ending CV. Always start each row from the prior period's ending CV.
Mistake #5: Thinking the $500,000 repayment at maturity is an "expense"
The $500,000 paid at maturity is a liability settlement, not an expense. Journal entry at maturity: Bonds Payable 500,000 / Cash 500,000. By this point, Discount = $0 because it was fully amortized.

9. Key Takeaways (6)

Takeaway #1 — Discount = Below Face
"At 96" = 96% of face = $480,000. Sold at discount because stated rate (5%) < market rate (~6%). Investors need compensation for accepting below-market coupon, so they pay less upfront.
Takeaway #2 — Discount is a Contra-Liability
Discount on Bonds Payable sits on the liability side as a deduction. Carrying Value = Face − Unamortized Discount. It starts at $480,000 and climbs to $500,000 over 10 periods.
Takeaway #3 — Interest Expense > Cash Interest (always for discounts)
Under both methods, Interest Expense exceeds the cash coupon payment. Under SL: $14,500 > $12,500. The extra $2,000 is the cost of borrowing at below-market rate. This makes economic sense.
Takeaway #4 — SL is Simpler, EIM is Theoretically Correct
SL gives a flat interest expense line. EIM gives a growing interest expense because the carrying value grows. EIM is preferred under US GAAP for material amounts; SL is acceptable and common on intro exams.
Takeaway #5 — Both Methods = Same Total Over 10 Periods
Total cash paid = $125,000 (10 × $12,500). Total discount amortized = $20,000. Total interest expense is approximately equal. The timing differs, not the total.
Takeaway #6 — At Maturity, Carrying Value Must Equal Face
This is your self-check. If your table doesn't end at exactly $500,000 CV and $0 unamortized discount, something went wrong. This is non-negotiable — the bond is repaid at face value.
