Cash Flow Statement — Explained Like You're in Middle School

Source: Trinity_Baek / Questions Board / "Explain to me like im in middle school" (2026-05-09)
Topic: Cash Flow Analysis (Rockefeller Company) — CFO + CFI + CFF + Depreciation when there's a sale

The Big Idea (1 sentence)

The Cash Flow Statement is a diary of where cash actually came in and went out during the year. The Income Statement says "we earned 250K," but the Cash Flow Statement says "yes — but here's exactly what happened to the actual money."

3 Buckets of Cash

1 CFO — Cash Flow from Operations
→ Money from the day-to-day business. Selling stuff, paying bills, paying taxes.
2 CFI — Cash Flow from Investing
→ Money from big purchases or sales. Buying a building, selling a truck, buying land.
3 CFF — Cash Flow from Financing
→ Money from banks, bondholders, shareholders. Borrowing, paying back loans, issuing stock, paying dividends.

The 3 buckets must add up to the change in Cash. Beginning Cash + (CFO + CFI + CFF) = Ending Cash.

Question 1 — How Much Dividend Was Paid?

Imagine your savings account. Beginning balance + deposits − withdrawals = Ending balance. Same logic for Retained Earnings.

Beginning RE + Net Income − Dividends = Ending RE
$300K + $250K − Dividends = $285K
Dividends Paid = $265K

Question 2 — Book Value & Gain on Sale of PP&E

The company sold a PP&E that originally cost $100K for $40K cash. The problem says the sale was $2K above book value.

Sale Price = Book Value + Gain
$40K = Book Value + $2K
→ Book Value = $38K

If the original Cost was $100K and the Book Value at sale was $38K, then the Accumulated Depreciation that piled up on this asset was:

Accumulated Depreciation on sold asset = Cost − Book Value = $100K − $38K = $62K
Gain on sale = $2K (small win — sold for slightly more than the depreciated value)

Question 3 — The Big One: Depreciation Expense

Why this is tricky: Normally Depreciation Expense = change in Accumulated Depreciation account. But when an asset was sold during the year, that simple rule breaks — because the sold asset's accumulated depreciation also gets removed from the account.

You have to do a T-account rollforward for Accumulated Depreciation:

Beginning A/D + Depreciation Expense (this year) − A/D removed (for sold asset) = Ending A/D
$200K + X − $62K = $270K
→ X = $132K
Depreciation Expense = $132K

5 Common Mistakes

  1. Forgetting that Gain on Sale needs to be subtracted from CFO. The gain is already in Net Income, but the cash from the sale shows up in CFI. If you don't subtract, you'd be double-counting.
  2. Treating Depreciation = ΔAccumulated Depreciation when there's a sale. The sold asset's A/D gets removed too — so the simple formula breaks. Always do a T-account rollforward.
  3. Confusing direction of working capital changes. Increase in asset (A/R up) = cash OUT. Increase in liability (A/P up) = cash IN.
  4. Mixing PP&E Cost with Net Book Value. The PP&E rollforward uses Cost ($100K removed), not Book Value ($38K).
  5. Forgetting Dividend = RE rollforward. Don't try to find dividends from the cash account directly — always do BB + NI − Div = EB.

6 Key Takeaways

  1. The Cash Flow Statement reconciles Net Income to actual cash. They are not the same thing.
  2. 3 sections: Operations (day-to-day), Investing (big assets), Financing (debt + equity).
  3. Indirect Method for CFO: Start with NI → add back non-cash → adjust for working capital changes.
  4. Non-cash items to add back: Depreciation, Amortization. Subtract gains (loss = add).
  5. When PP&E is sold, do both rollforwards (PP&E cost AND Accumulated Depreciation) to find missing values.
  6. Sanity check: Beginning Cash + (CFO + CFI + CFF) = Ending Cash. If this doesn't balance, something is wrong.

Quick Mental Model

Think of the company as a person with 3 wallets:

  • Wallet 1 — Job money (CFO): salary, bills, groceries
  • Wallet 2 — Big stuff (CFI): bought a car, sold an old TV
  • Wallet 3 — Bank stuff (CFF): took a loan, paid loan, gave parents an allowance

End of year, count what's left in Wallet 1 + 2 + 3 vs what you started with. That's your Cash Flow Statement.
