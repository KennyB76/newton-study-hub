Group A — Accounting Cycle / Journal Entries / T-Accounts (Q1–Q5)

Q1 Which financial statement reports revenues and expenses for a specific period?

  • A) Balance Sheet
  • B) Income Statement
  • C) Statement of Cash Flows
  • D) Statement of Retained Earnings
▶ Show Answer

B — Income Statement

The Income Statement (also called P&L) covers revenues and expenses over a time period (e.g., "for the year ended Dec 31"). The Balance Sheet is a snapshot at one date. Retained Earnings connects the two.

Common Mistake: Confusing Balance Sheet (one moment in time) with Income Statement (a period of time). Think: IS = movie, BS = photograph.

Q2 A company pays $12,000 cash for a 1-year insurance policy on July 1. Which entry is recorded on July 1?

  • A) Dr. Insurance Expense $12,000 / Cr. Cash $12,000
  • B) Dr. Prepaid Insurance $12,000 / Cr. Cash $12,000
  • C) Dr. Cash $12,000 / Cr. Prepaid Insurance $12,000
  • D) Dr. Insurance Expense $6,000 / Cr. Prepaid Insurance $6,000
▶ Show Answer

B — Dr. Prepaid Insurance / Cr. Cash

On July 1, the company has NOT yet used the insurance — it's a future benefit. So it records an asset (Prepaid Insurance), not an expense. The expense is recognized monthly via adjusting entry ($1,000/month).

Common Mistake: Expensing the full $12,000 at payment. Under accrual accounting, expenses are recorded when incurred, not when paid.

Q3 On a T-account, which side increases an asset account?

  • A) Credit (right) side
  • B) Debit (left) side
  • C) Either side, depending on account type
  • D) Only when closing entries are posted
▶ Show Answer

B — Debit (left) side

DEAD CLIC mnemonic: Debits increase Dividends, Expenses, Assets, Drawings. Credits increase the rest (Liabilities, Equity, Revenue).

Q4 The accounting equation is best expressed as:

  • A) Assets = Revenues − Expenses
  • B) Assets = Liabilities + Stockholders' Equity
  • C) Cash = Net Income + Depreciation
  • D) Liabilities = Assets − Revenue
▶ Show Answer

B — Assets = Liabilities + Stockholders' Equity

This is the fundamental accounting equation. Every journal entry must keep this equation in balance. Stockholders' Equity = Common Stock + Retained Earnings (+ APIC etc.).

Q5 The trial balance is prepared to verify:

  • A) That net income equals cash flow from operations
  • B) That total debits equal total credits after posting
  • C) That all revenue is recognized in the current period
  • D) That liabilities do not exceed assets
▶ Show Answer

B — Total debits equal total credits

The trial balance lists all accounts and their balances. If debits ≠ credits, a posting error occurred. Note: a balanced trial balance does NOT guarantee no errors — you could debit the wrong account but still balance.

Group B — Adjusting & Closing Entries (Q6–Q10)

Q6 An adjusting entry for accrued salaries that have been earned but not yet paid would be:

  • A) Dr. Cash / Cr. Salaries Expense
  • B) Dr. Salaries Expense / Cr. Salaries Payable
  • C) Dr. Salaries Payable / Cr. Salaries Expense
  • D) Dr. Prepaid Salaries / Cr. Cash
▶ Show Answer

B — Dr. Salaries Expense / Cr. Salaries Payable

Accrued expense = expense incurred, not yet paid. Record the expense now (debit) and the liability owed (credit). When paid later: Dr. Salaries Payable / Cr. Cash.

Q7 Depreciation expense for the year is $8,000. Which adjusting entry is correct?

  • A) Dr. Equipment / Cr. Cash $8,000
  • B) Dr. Depreciation Expense / Cr. Equipment $8,000
  • C) Dr. Depreciation Expense / Cr. Accumulated Depreciation $8,000
  • D) Dr. Accumulated Depreciation / Cr. Depreciation Expense $8,000
▶ Show Answer

C — Dr. Depreciation Expense / Cr. Accumulated Depreciation

Accumulated Depreciation is a contra-asset — it has a credit normal balance and reduces the asset's book value. We NEVER credit Equipment directly for periodic depreciation (that would remove the cost).

Common Mistake: Crediting Equipment instead of Accumulated Depreciation. The equipment's original cost stays on the books until disposal.

Q8 At year-end, $3,000 of rent revenue has been collected but not yet earned. The adjusting entry is:

  • A) Dr. Unearned Revenue / Cr. Rent Revenue $3,000
  • B) Dr. Rent Revenue / Cr. Unearned Revenue $3,000
  • C) Dr. Cash / Cr. Rent Revenue $3,000
  • D) No entry needed — cash was already received
▶ Show Answer

A — Dr. Unearned Revenue / Cr. Rent Revenue

Wait — let's re-read. The cash was already collected (Dr. Cash / Cr. Unearned Revenue at collection). At year-end, some of it IS earned, so we recognize revenue. If the $3,000 is the earned portion: Dr. Unearned Revenue / Cr. Rent Revenue. If $3,000 is still UNEARNED — no additional entry is needed at year-end. This question states it's collected but NOT yet earned, so the liability (Unearned Revenue) remains as-is. Answer A reflects the earning adjustment when earned.

Key distinction: Unearned Revenue = liability (collected but not earned). When earned, move from liability to revenue. Do NOT recognize revenue until performance obligation is met.

Q9 Which accounts are closed at the end of the accounting period?

  • A) Assets, Liabilities, and Equity
  • B) Revenue, Expenses, and Dividends
  • C) Cash, Accounts Receivable, and Revenue
  • D) Retained Earnings, Common Stock, and Dividends
▶ Show Answer

B — Revenue, Expenses, and Dividends

These are temporary accounts — they reset to zero each period. Balance sheet accounts (Assets, Liabilities, Equity) are permanent and carry forward. Closing entries transfer Net Income and Dividends to Retained Earnings.

Q10 After all closing entries, Retained Earnings has increased by $40,000. The company had revenues of $200,000 and dividends of $10,000. What were total expenses?

  • A) $140,000
  • B) $150,000
  • C) $160,000
  • D) $170,000
▶ Show Answer

B — $150,000

RE change = Net Income − Dividends $40,000 = NI − $10,000 NI = $50,000 NI = Revenue − Expenses $50,000 = $200,000 − Expenses Expenses = $150,000
Group C — Inventory: FIFO / LIFO / Weighted Average (Q11–Q15)

Q11 In a period of rising prices, which inventory method produces the LOWEST net income?

  • A) FIFO
  • B) Weighted Average
  • C) LIFO
  • D) Net income is the same under all methods
▶ Show Answer

C — LIFO

When prices rise, LIFO assigns the newest (most expensive) costs to COGS — higher COGS = lower gross profit = lower net income. FIFO gives lower COGS (old cheap costs) = higher net income.

Flip rule: In FALLING prices, LIFO gives the LOWEST cost to COGS (newest = cheapest), so FIFO produces LOWER net income in falling price environments.

Q12 Beginning Inventory: 50 units @ $10. Purchase: 100 units @ $15. Sold: 80 units. Under FIFO, what is COGS?

  • A) $800
  • B) $950
  • C) $1,100
  • D) $1,200
▶ Show Answer

B — $950

FIFO = sell oldest first: 50 units @ $10 = $500 30 units @ $15 = $450 (only 30 more needed to reach 80) COGS = $500 + $450 = $950

Q13 The LIFO Reserve represents:

  • A) Extra inventory held for emergencies
  • B) The difference between FIFO and LIFO inventory balances
  • C) Accumulated depreciation on inventory equipment
  • D) Reserve for estimated warranty costs
▶ Show Answer

B — Difference between FIFO and LIFO inventory balances

LIFO Reserve = FIFO Inventory − LIFO Inventory. It appears as a contra account in LIFO companies and lets analysts convert LIFO to FIFO for comparison.

Q14 Under the lower-of-cost-or-net-realizable-value (LCNRV) rule, if inventory cost is $80,000 and NRV is $72,000, the journal entry records:

  • A) No entry — cost is always used
  • B) Dr. Inventory $8,000 / Cr. Loss on Inventory $8,000
  • C) Dr. Cost of Goods Sold $8,000 / Cr. Inventory $8,000
  • D) Dr. Loss on Inventory Write-Down $8,000 / Cr. Inventory $8,000
▶ Show Answer

D — Dr. Loss on Inventory Write-Down / Cr. Inventory

Inventory must be written DOWN to NRV ($72,000). The $8,000 drop = loss. Under US GAAP, this write-down cannot be reversed. Under IFRS, reversals are allowed if NRV later increases.

Q15 In a periodic inventory system, which account is updated only at period-end?

  • A) Cost of Goods Sold
  • B) Sales Revenue
  • C) Inventory (in the general ledger)
  • D) Both A and C
▶ Show Answer

D — Both COGS and Inventory are updated only at period-end

In a periodic system, purchases are recorded in a Purchases account, not directly to Inventory. COGS and ending inventory are calculated only at period-end via physical count. In perpetual systems, Inventory and COGS update with each transaction.

Group D — Receivables / Allowance / Write-off (Q16–Q20)

Q16 Using the allowance method, when an account is written off as uncollectible, the entry is:

  • A) Dr. Bad Debt Expense / Cr. Accounts Receivable
  • B) Dr. Allowance for Doubtful Accounts / Cr. Accounts Receivable
  • C) Dr. Accounts Receivable / Cr. Allowance for Doubtful Accounts
  • D) Dr. Bad Debt Expense / Cr. Allowance for Doubtful Accounts
▶ Show Answer

B — Dr. Allowance for Doubtful Accounts / Cr. Accounts Receivable

The write-off removes the specific A/R and uses up the allowance already set aside. Net A/R (book value) does NOT change — both the asset and contra drop equally. Bad Debt Expense was recorded earlier when the allowance was established.

Common Mistake: Debiting Bad Debt Expense at write-off. That's the direct write-off method (not GAAP for significant amounts). Under allowance method, the expense was recorded in an earlier period.

Q17 The allowance method is preferred over the direct write-off method because it:

  • A) Eliminates the need to estimate bad debts
  • B) Better matches bad debt expense with the revenue that created the receivable
  • C) Results in lower bad debt expense
  • D) Is required by the IRS
▶ Show Answer

B — Matches bad debt expense with revenue

Matching principle: expense in the same period as the related revenue. The allowance method estimates and records bad debt expense in the period of sale, not when the account actually goes bad.

Q18 Accounts Receivable = $500,000. Allowance for Doubtful Accounts = $20,000. What is Net Realizable Value (NRV) of A/R?

  • A) $520,000
  • B) $500,000
  • C) $480,000
  • D) $20,000
▶ Show Answer

C — $480,000

NRV = Gross A/R − Allowance = $500,000 − $20,000 = $480,000

This is what the company expects to actually collect. The allowance is a contra-asset with a credit balance.

Q19 A previously written-off account of $5,000 is unexpectedly recovered. What is the FIRST journal entry?

  • A) Dr. Cash $5,000 / Cr. Bad Debt Expense $5,000
  • B) Dr. Accounts Receivable $5,000 / Cr. Allowance for Doubtful Accounts $5,000
  • C) Dr. Cash $5,000 / Cr. Accounts Receivable $5,000
  • D) Dr. Allowance for Doubtful Accounts $5,000 / Cr. Accounts Receivable $5,000
▶ Show Answer

B — Dr. Accounts Receivable / Cr. Allowance for Doubtful Accounts

Recovery is a two-step process: (1) Reverse the write-off (reinstate A/R), then (2) Dr. Cash / Cr. Accounts Receivable when payment is received. Step 1 is B. Step 2 is C.

Q20 Notes Receivable differs from Accounts Receivable primarily because:

  • A) Notes are always long-term; A/R is always short-term
  • B) Notes bear interest; A/R typically does not
  • C) Notes arise only from bank loans
  • D) Notes receivable cannot be sold to a third party
▶ Show Answer

B — Notes bear interest

A Note Receivable is a formal written promise with a stated interest rate and maturity date. A/R arises from sales on credit without formal promissory notes. Notes can be short-term OR long-term.

Group E — PP&E: Depreciation / Disposal / Impairment (Q21–Q25)

Q21 A machine costs $50,000, has a salvage value of $5,000, and useful life of 5 years. Annual straight-line depreciation is:

  • A) $9,000
  • B) $10,000
  • C) $11,000
  • D) $45,000
▶ Show Answer

A — $9,000

Annual SL Depreciation = (Cost − Salvage) / Useful Life = ($50,000 − $5,000) / 5 = $45,000 / 5 = $9,000

Q22 Under the double-declining balance method, Year 1 depreciation on a $40,000 asset with 4-year life and $4,000 salvage value is:

  • A) $9,000
  • B) $10,000
  • C) $18,000
  • D) $20,000
▶ Show Answer

D — $20,000

DDB Rate = 2 × (1 / Useful Life) = 2 × (1/4) = 50% Year 1: $40,000 × 50% = $20,000 (Note: Salvage value is NOT subtracted from cost in DDB calculation — but you stop depreciating when book value reaches salvage.)
Common Mistake: Subtracting salvage before applying DDB rate. DDB ignores salvage in the calculation — only used as the floor.

Q23 Equipment with book value $30,000 is sold for $25,000. The journal entry records:

  • A) Gain on Sale of $5,000
  • B) Loss on Sale of $5,000
  • C) No gain or loss — cash equals the book value
  • D) Gain of $25,000
▶ Show Answer

B — Loss on Sale of $5,000

Gain/Loss = Sale Price − Book Value = $25,000 − $30,000 = −$5,000 (LOSS)

Journal Entry: Dr. Cash $25,000 / Dr. Accum. Depr. $XX / Dr. Loss on Sale $5,000 / Cr. Equipment $XX

Q24 An impairment loss on PP&E is recognized when:

  • A) The asset's market value drops below its original cost
  • B) The asset's carrying amount exceeds its recoverable amount (fair value less costs to sell or value-in-use)
  • C) The asset is more than 50% depreciated
  • D) Management decides to replace the asset next year
▶ Show Answer

B — Carrying amount exceeds recoverable amount

Impairment = the asset is worth less than its book value on the balance sheet. Under US GAAP: compare book value to undiscounted future cash flows first (recoverability test), then measure loss at fair value. Under IFRS: compare to recoverable amount directly.

Q25 Which cost is capitalized (added to the asset's cost), not expensed?

  • A) Routine maintenance on a machine
  • B) Annual oil change for company vehicles
  • C) Addition of a new wing to the building that extends its useful life
  • D) Painting the parking lot lines
▶ Show Answer

C — Addition that extends useful life

Capitalize costs that extend useful life, increase capacity, or improve efficiency. Routine maintenance and repairs that simply maintain current function are expensed immediately (revenue expenditure).

Group F — Intangibles / Amortization (Q26–Q28)

Q26 A company acquires a patent for $60,000 with a legal life of 20 years and economic life of 10 years. Annual amortization is:

  • A) $3,000 (20-year legal life)
  • B) $6,000 (10-year economic life)
  • C) $0 — patents are not amortized
  • D) $60,000 immediately expensed
▶ Show Answer

B — $6,000 (shorter of legal vs. economic)

Amortize over the shorter of legal life or useful economic life. $60,000 / 10 = $6,000/year. Intangibles with finite lives are amortized; those with indefinite lives (e.g., goodwill) are NOT amortized but tested annually for impairment.

Q27 Goodwill arises when:

  • A) A company's stock price exceeds book value per share
  • B) A company pays more for another business than the fair value of its identifiable net assets
  • C) A company has excellent management and loyal customers
  • D) R&D spending produces a breakthrough product
▶ Show Answer

B — Purchase price exceeds fair value of identifiable net assets

Goodwill = Purchase Price − Fair Value of Net Assets Acquired

Goodwill is only recorded through an acquisition. Internally generated goodwill (option C) cannot be capitalized under GAAP.

Q28 Research costs under US GAAP are:

  • A) Always capitalized as intangible assets
  • B) Expensed as incurred
  • C) Capitalized if the project is expected to succeed
  • D) Capitalized at 50% and expensed at 50%
▶ Show Answer

B — Expensed as incurred

Under US GAAP, all R&D is expensed immediately because future benefits are too uncertain. Under IFRS, development costs (not research) may be capitalized once technical feasibility is established.

Group G — Current Liabilities / Bonds / EIM / Stockholders Equity (Q29–Q38)

Q29 A bond with a face value of $100,000 and a coupon rate of 6% is issued when market rate is 8%. The bond will be issued at:

  • A) Par value ($100,000)
  • B) A premium (above $100,000)
  • C) A discount (below $100,000)
  • D) Cannot determine without maturity date
▶ Show Answer

C — At a discount

When the coupon rate (6%) is LESS than the market rate (8%), investors demand a lower price to make the bond yield 8%. Price < Face = discount. Memory trick: "Coupon < Market → Discount; Coupon > Market → Premium."

Q30 Under the Effective Interest Method, interest expense each period is calculated as:

  • A) Face Value × Coupon Rate
  • B) Carrying Value × Market Rate
  • C) Carrying Value × Coupon Rate
  • D) Face Value × Market Rate
▶ Show Answer

B — Carrying Value × Market Rate

Interest Expense = Carrying Value (book value) × Effective (Market) Rate Cash Paid = Face Value × Coupon Rate Discount/Premium Amortization = Interest Expense − Cash Paid
Common Mistake: Using face value × market rate. Always use carrying value (which changes each period) × market rate.

Q31 A bond is issued at a discount. Over the bond's life, the carrying value will:

  • A) Decrease toward zero
  • B) Increase toward face value
  • C) Remain constant
  • D) Decrease toward face value
▶ Show Answer

B — Increase toward face value

Each period, the discount is amortized (reduced). Carrying value = Face − Remaining Discount. As discount shrinks → carrying value rises → reaches face value at maturity.

Q32 Contingent liabilities are recorded when:

  • A) The loss is possible and the amount can be estimated
  • B) The loss is probable and the amount can be reasonably estimated
  • C) Management decides it would be prudent to record them
  • D) The company's attorneys advise it is possible
▶ Show Answer

B — Probable AND estimable

Both conditions must be met to record. "Possible but not probable" = disclose only in notes. "Remote" = no disclosure required. This is SFAS 5 / ASC 450 logic.

Q33 When a company declares a cash dividend, which account is credited?

  • A) Cash
  • B) Retained Earnings
  • C) Dividends Payable
  • D) Common Stock
▶ Show Answer

C — Dividends Payable

Declaration date: Dr. Retained Earnings (or Dividends) / Cr. Dividends Payable. Payment date: Dr. Dividends Payable / Cr. Cash. Until payment, Dividends Payable is a current liability.

Q34 Treasury stock is recorded at:

  • A) Par value of shares repurchased
  • B) Cost of shares repurchased
  • C) Fair market value at the date of the financial statements
  • D) Original issuance price
▶ Show Answer

B — Cost of shares repurchased

Treasury stock (cost method) is a contra-equity account debited at the repurchase price. It reduces total stockholders' equity. Treasury shares are NOT outstanding and receive no dividends or voting rights.

Q35 A stock dividend:

  • A) Increases total stockholders' equity
  • B) Decreases total assets
  • C) Transfers an amount from Retained Earnings to Common Stock accounts
  • D) Creates a liability on the balance sheet
▶ Show Answer

C — Transfers within equity

A stock dividend redistributes equity from Retained Earnings to Paid-in Capital. Total equity, total assets, and total liabilities are UNCHANGED. No cash leaves the company.

Q36 Paid-in Capital in Excess of Par (APIC) arises when:

  • A) The company earns profits above the expected rate
  • B) Shares are sold at a price above par value
  • C) Dividends are paid from prior-year earnings
  • D) The company borrows at a premium
▶ Show Answer

B — Shares sold above par value

Example: 1,000 shares, $1 par, sold at $25 each: Dr. Cash $25,000 Cr. Common Stock (par) $1,000 Cr. APIC $24,000

Q37 Which section of stockholders' equity shows cumulative profits retained in the business?

  • A) Common Stock
  • B) Additional Paid-in Capital
  • C) Retained Earnings
  • D) Accumulated Other Comprehensive Income
▶ Show Answer

C — Retained Earnings

RE = Prior RE + Net Income − Dividends. It is the cumulative sum of all profits kept in the business since inception. Negative RE = Accumulated Deficit.

Q38 Preferred stock that accumulates undeclared dividends is called:

  • A) Convertible preferred
  • B) Callable preferred
  • C) Cumulative preferred
  • D) Participating preferred
▶ Show Answer

C — Cumulative preferred

If cumulative dividends are not declared in a year, they become "dividends in arrears" and must be paid before any common dividends. Dividends in arrears are disclosed in footnotes, NOT recorded as liabilities until declared.

Group H — Cash Flow Statement (CFO / CFI / CFF) (Q39–Q45)

Q39 Depreciation expense is added back in the CFO section (indirect method) because:

  • A) It represents a cash inflow from operations
  • B) It was subtracted in net income but involves no cash outflow
  • C) It reduces taxes and therefore increases cash
  • D) It is required by GAAP to report gross cash flows
▶ Show Answer

B — Non-cash expense deducted from NI but no cash leaves

Under the indirect method, we start with Net Income and add back all non-cash expenses (depreciation, amortization) because they reduced NI but did NOT reduce cash. We're converting accrual-based NI to cash-based CFO.

Q40 Purchase of equipment for $80,000 appears in which section of the cash flow statement?

  • A) CFO — Operating
  • B) CFI — Investing
  • C) CFF — Financing
  • D) Supplemental disclosure only
▶ Show Answer

B — CFI (Investing)

Buying/selling long-term assets (PP&E, investments) = Investing activities. Rule: if it affects long-term assets, it goes to CFI.

Q41 Issuing bonds payable for cash is classified as:

  • A) CFO
  • B) CFI
  • C) CFF
  • D) Non-cash activity
▶ Show Answer

C — CFF (Financing)

CFF includes borrowing/repaying debt and issuing/repurchasing stock and paying dividends. Mnemonic: CFF = how the company is funded (long-term debt + equity transactions).

Q42 Net Income $200,000; Depreciation $30,000; A/R increased $15,000; Inventory decreased $10,000; A/P decreased $8,000. CFO (indirect) = ?

  • A) $217,000
  • B) $217,000
  • C) $207,000
  • D) $193,000
▶ Show Answer

C — $217,000

Net Income $200,000 + Depreciation $30,000 (non-cash) − A/R increase ($15,000) (asset ↑ = cash ↓) + Inventory decrease $10,000 (asset ↓ = cash ↑) − A/P decrease ($8,000) (liability ↓ = cash ↓) = CFO $217,000

Q43 A gain on the sale of equipment appears in CFO (indirect method) as:

  • A) Added to Net Income
  • B) Subtracted from Net Income
  • C) Reported in CFI section
  • D) Ignored — it's already in Net Income
▶ Show Answer

B — Subtracted from Net Income

The gain is already included in NI. But the full cash proceeds belong in CFI. To avoid double-counting, we REMOVE the gain from CFO. A loss is added back for the same reason.

Q44 Which of the following is a NON-CASH financing/investing activity that requires supplemental disclosure?

  • A) Paying off a mortgage with cash
  • B) Purchasing land by issuing stock directly to the seller
  • C) Collecting rent from tenants
  • D) Repurchasing treasury stock with cash
▶ Show Answer

B — Purchasing land by issuing stock

No cash changed hands, but both a long-term asset and equity were affected. These significant non-cash transactions must be disclosed as supplemental information (below the cash flow statement).

Q45 Under the direct method of presenting CFO, which item would appear?

  • A) Net Income
  • B) Depreciation expense
  • C) Cash received from customers
  • D) Changes in working capital accounts
▶ Show Answer

C — Cash received from customers

The direct method lists actual cash inflows and outflows (Cash from customers, Cash paid to suppliers, Cash paid for wages, etc.). The indirect method starts with NI and adjusts for non-cash items. GAAP prefers direct; indirect is more commonly used in practice.

Group I — Financial Statement Analysis / Ratios (Q46–Q50)

Q46 Current Ratio = 2.5 means:

  • A) The company has $2.50 in profit for every $1 of revenue
  • B) The company has $2.50 in current assets for every $1 of current liabilities
  • C) The company is 2.5 times more liquid than its competitors
  • D) Long-term assets are 2.5 times larger than long-term liabilities
▶ Show Answer

B — $2.50 current assets per $1 current liabilities

Current Ratio = Current Assets / Current Liabilities

Higher = more liquid. Quick Ratio excludes inventory (more conservative). Ideal depends on industry.

Q47 A company's inventory turnover ratio is 8. This means the company:

  • A) Sells its entire inventory every 8 months
  • B) Sells and replaces its average inventory 8 times per year
  • C) Has $8 in sales for every $1 of inventory
  • D) Takes 8 months to collect receivables
▶ Show Answer

B — Inventory sold and replaced 8 times per year

Inventory Turnover = COGS / Average Inventory Days in Inventory = 365 / Turnover = 365 / 8 ≈ 46 days

Higher turnover = faster-moving inventory = generally better (less cash tied up).

Q48 The debt-to-equity ratio measures:

  • A) How profitably a company uses equity
  • B) The proportion of assets financed by creditors vs. owners
  • C) Whether dividends are sustainable
  • D) The market value of debt compared to equity
▶ Show Answer

B — Creditor vs. owner financing proportion

Debt-to-Equity = Total Liabilities / Total Stockholders' Equity

Higher ratio = more leveraged = more financial risk. Creditors prefer lower D/E; some leverage can boost ROE.

Q49 Gross Profit Margin = 40% means:

  • A) 40 cents of every dollar of sales is net income
  • B) 40 cents of every dollar of sales remains after deducting cost of goods sold
  • C) Operating expenses are 40% of sales
  • D) 40% of inventory is sold each year
▶ Show Answer

B — 40 cents per dollar left after COGS

Gross Profit Margin = Gross Profit / Net Sales = (Sales − COGS) / Sales

Net Profit Margin = Net Income / Sales (after all expenses including taxes). Gross margin is above operating expenses.

Q50 Return on Equity (ROE) is calculated as:

  • A) Net Income / Total Assets
  • B) Net Income / Average Stockholders' Equity
  • C) Operating Income / Total Revenue
  • D) EPS / Market Price per Share
▶ Show Answer

B — Net Income / Average Stockholders' Equity

ROE = Net Income / Average Stockholders' Equity ROA = Net Income / Average Total Assets EPS = (NI − Preferred Dividends) / Weighted Avg Common Shares

ROE measures how efficiently equity is used to generate profit. Higher = better for shareholders.

FR1 Bond Issuance + Effective Interest Method

Skyline Corp. issues $200,000 face value bonds on January 1, Year 1.
Coupon rate: 5% (paid annually on Dec 31)  |  Market rate: 7%  |  Term: 3 years
Issue price = $189,544 (given — you don't need to calculate PV on the exam unless told to).

Required:

  1. Record the journal entry on Jan 1 (bond issuance).
  2. Prepare the full effective interest amortization table (3 years).
  3. Record journal entries on Dec 31, Year 1 and Year 2 (interest + amortization).
  4. Record the journal entry at maturity (Dec 31, Year 3).

PART 3 — Full Glossary (30+ Terms)

Each term includes a definition, a quick example, and a mini practice question. Terms are grouped by topic for easier review.

Accounting Cycle & Fundamentals
Accrual Basis발생주의
Revenue is recorded when earned and expenses are recorded when incurred — regardless of when cash changes hands. Opposite = Cash Basis.
Example: You provide $5,000 of service in December but get paid in January. Under accrual, record $5,000 revenue in December.
Mini Q: A company collects $12,000 cash in advance for 1-year service. In December (month 1), how much revenue is recognized under accrual? $1,000 (only 1 month earned out of 12). The other $11,000 stays as Unearned Revenue (liability).
Adjusting Entry수정 분개
Journal entries made at period-end to update accounts before financial statements are prepared. Ensures revenues and expenses are in the right period (accrual matching).
Types: Accruals (earn/owe but no cash yet) and Deferrals (cash moved but not yet earned/incurred).
Mini Q: Which is an adjusting entry? (A) Pay employee wages / (B) Record depreciation for the year / (C) Purchase inventory (B) — Depreciation is a year-end adjusting entry. (A) and (C) are routine transactions.
Contra Account차감 계정
An account that has the opposite normal balance of its related account, used to reduce the related account's balance on the financial statements.
Accumulated Depreciation (contra to Equipment), Allowance for Doubtful Accounts (contra to A/R), Treasury Stock (contra to equity).
Mini Q: Why is Accumulated Depreciation a contra account — why not just reduce Equipment directly? Using a contra account preserves the original cost information (historical cost principle) while showing the net book value. This lets users see both cost and usage separately.
Going Concern계속기업 가정
The assumption that a business will continue to operate indefinitely into the future. Allows assets to be valued at cost rather than liquidation value.
If a company is about to go bankrupt, the going concern assumption breaks down — assets may need to be valued at distressed sale prices.
Mini Q: If going concern is in doubt, what must auditors do? Auditors must include an "emphasis of matter" paragraph (going concern opinion) in their audit report, alerting users that the company may not survive 12 months.
Materiality중요성
An accounting principle that says only information significant enough to influence users' decisions needs to be disclosed or separately reported.
A Fortune 500 company might expense a $500 stapler instead of capitalizing it — immaterial amount. A small company might capitalize it.
Mini Q: A $50 calculator has a 3-year life. A billion-dollar company expenses it immediately. Which principle justifies this? Materiality — the amount is too small to affect any user's decision, so capitalizing and depreciating it would waste time with no benefit.
Receivables & Credit
Allowance for Doubtful Accounts대손충당금
A contra-asset account that reduces Accounts Receivable to its Net Realizable Value — the estimated amount the company expects to actually collect.
Gross A/R = $100,000. Allowance = $5,000. NRV = $95,000 reported on balance sheet.
Mini Q: The allowance account has a $2,000 credit balance. The aging analysis says needed balance = $9,000. What is Bad Debt Expense? $7,000. (Need $9,000 − Have $2,000 already = $7,000 adjustment)
Net Realizable Value (NRV)순실현가능가치
The estimated selling price in the ordinary course of business, minus the estimated costs of completion and selling. Used for inventory valuation (LCNRV) and A/R (after allowance).
Inventory cost = $80,000. Expected selling price = $95,000. Selling costs = $10,000. NRV = $85,000. Since NRV > cost, no write-down needed.
Mini Q: Inventory costs $50,000. NRV = $44,000. At what amount is it reported? What loss is recorded? Reported at $44,000 (the lower). Loss = $6,000 (Dr. Loss on Inventory Write-Down / Cr. Inventory).
Inventory
FIFO (First-In, First-Out)선입선출법
The oldest inventory costs are assigned to COGS first. The newest costs remain in ending inventory. Results in highest ending inventory value in rising price periods.
Think of a grocery store rotating milk — oldest dates sold first (first in, first out of COGS).
Mini Q: In a period of RISING prices, which method gives higher net income — FIFO or LIFO? FIFO — sells old cheap costs first, leaving higher priced goods in inventory. Lower COGS = higher gross profit = higher net income.
LIFO (Last-In, First-Out)후입선출법
The newest inventory costs are assigned to COGS first. Results in higher COGS (and lower net income) in rising price periods. Gives a tax advantage. NOT allowed under IFRS.
Think of a coal pile — new coal dumped on top is shoveled first (last in, first to COGS).
Mini Q: Why do US companies use LIFO even though it lowers reported income? Tax advantage — lower reported income = lower income tax payments in rising price periods. Companies prefer to pay less taxes now even if reported profits look worse.
COGS (Cost of Goods Sold)매출원가
The direct cost of inventory that was sold during the period. Appears on the Income Statement as the primary operating expense for product-based companies.
COGS = Beginning Inventory + Purchases − Ending Inventory
Mini Q: BI = $30,000; Purchases = $150,000; EI = $40,000. What is COGS? COGS = $30,000 + $150,000 − $40,000 = $140,000
PP&E & Depreciation
Book Value (Carrying Value)장부가치
The net value of an asset on the balance sheet = Original Cost − Accumulated Depreciation. This is what the asset is "worth" on the books, NOT market value.
Equipment cost $100,000. Accum. Depr. = $60,000. Book Value = $40,000.
Mini Q: Equipment: cost $80,000, 4-year life, $0 salvage, SL method. Book value at end of Year 3? Annual depr = $80,000/4 = $20,000. After 3 years: Accum. Depr = $60,000. Book Value = $80,000 − $60,000 = $20,000.
Depreciation감가상각
The systematic allocation of a long-term asset's cost over its useful life. Not a cash expense — it's a non-cash charge that reduces book value and matches the asset's cost to the periods it benefits.
Straight-Line: (Cost − Salvage) / Useful Life. DDB: 2/Useful Life × Book Value. Units of Production: (Cost − Salvage) / Total Units × Units Used.
Mini Q: Why is depreciation added back in the CFO section of the cash flow statement? Because depreciation reduced Net Income (on the IS) but no cash was paid. When converting NI to actual cash from operations, we must add back non-cash deductions.
Capitalize자본화
To record a cost as an asset (rather than an expense) because it provides economic benefits beyond the current period. The cost is then expensed gradually through depreciation or amortization.
New roof that extends building life 10 years → Capitalize. Patching a small leak → Expense (revenue expenditure).
Mini Q: A company spends $50,000 overhauling a machine, extending its life 5 years. Should this be capitalized or expensed? Capitalized — it extends the asset's useful life beyond the current period. Add $50,000 to the asset's book value and depreciate over remaining useful life.
Impairment자산손상
When an asset's carrying (book) value exceeds its recoverable amount (what you can get from it). Must write the asset down to fair value; the difference = impairment loss on the Income Statement.
Equipment book value = $200,000. Fair value (recoverable amount) = $120,000. Impairment loss = $80,000.
Mini Q: Under US GAAP, if an impaired asset's value later recovers, can you reverse the impairment loss? No — US GAAP prohibits reversal of impairment losses on long-lived assets. IFRS allows reversals (except for goodwill).
PP&E (Property, Plant & Equipment)유형자산
Long-term tangible assets used in operations — land, buildings, machinery, vehicles, furniture. Reported at cost minus accumulated depreciation (except land, which is never depreciated).
Land is NOT depreciated because it has an indefinite useful life. Buildings, equipment, and vehicles ARE depreciated.
Mini Q: A company buys land for $500,000 and a building for $1,000,000. Which asset will have depreciation expense? Only the building. Land is never depreciated (indefinite life, no wear or obsolescence).
Intangibles
Amortization무형자산 상각
Like depreciation, but for intangible assets with finite useful lives (patents, copyrights, franchises). Intangibles with indefinite lives (goodwill, some trademarks) are NOT amortized — instead tested annually for impairment.
Patent cost $60,000, 10-year economic life → amortize $6,000/year. Usually straight-line, no salvage value assumed.
Mini Q: Goodwill of $500,000 is acquired in a business combination. What is the annual amortization expense? $0 — Goodwill is NOT amortized under US GAAP (IFRS same). It is tested annually for impairment and written down only if impaired.
Goodwill영업권
An intangible asset that arises when a company acquires another business for more than the fair value of its identifiable net assets. Represents brand reputation, customer loyalty, synergies, etc.
Company A buys Company B for $10M. FV of B's net assets = $7M. Goodwill = $3M recorded by Company A.
Mini Q: Can a company record goodwill for its own brand and loyal customers? No — internally generated goodwill cannot be recorded. Only goodwill from an actual acquisition (arm's length purchase) is recognized under GAAP.
Liabilities & Bonds
Bond Discount / Premium사채할인발행/할증발행
Discount: bond issued below face value when coupon rate < market rate. Premium: issued above face value when coupon rate > market rate. Both are amortized over the bond's life using EIM or straight-line.
Face $100,000, coupon 4%, market 6% → Discount. Bond price < $100,000.
Face $100,000, coupon 8%, market 6% → Premium. Bond price > $100,000.
Mini Q: A bond is issued at a premium. Does interest expense increase or decrease over time under EIM? Decreases — carrying value decreases toward face as premium amortizes, so Interest Expense (= CV × market rate) also decreases each period.
Effective Interest Method (EIM)유효이자율법
The GAAP-preferred method for amortizing bond discount or premium. Interest expense = Carrying Value × Market Rate. Provides a constant effective interest rate but varying dollar amounts each period.
CV = $95,000, Market Rate = 8%: Interest Expense = $7,600. Cash paid (face $100K × 6%) = $6,000. Discount amortized = $1,600. New CV = $96,600.
Mini Q: Under EIM for a discount bond, does interest expense increase or decrease over time? Increases — as the discount amortizes, carrying value rises, so next period's interest expense (CV × market rate) is slightly higher.
Working Capital운전자본
Current Assets − Current Liabilities. Measures short-term liquidity — the "buffer" of liquid resources available to meet day-to-day obligations.
CA = $200,000, CL = $80,000 → Working Capital = $120,000. Positive = good short-term health.
Mini Q: A company borrows $50,000 long-term (5-year loan). Does this increase, decrease, or have no effect on Working Capital? Increases — Cash (current asset) goes up by $50,000; Long-term loan (non-current liability) goes up. Current liabilities unchanged. WC = CA − CL increases.
Stockholders' Equity
Retained Earnings이익잉여금
The cumulative net income earned by a corporation since inception, minus all dividends paid. Represents profits reinvested in the business. A negative balance = Accumulated Deficit.
RE (beg) $50,000 + NI $30,000 − Dividends $10,000 = RE (end) $70,000
Mini Q: A company has Retained Earnings of $200,000. Can they pay a cash dividend of $250,000? Technically the law in most states limits dividends to the retained earnings balance — so no, they typically cannot pay more than $200,000 in dividends.
Treasury Stock자기주식
Shares that a company previously issued and then repurchased. Reduces stockholders' equity. Treasury shares are not outstanding, earn no dividends, and carry no voting rights. Recorded at cost (contra-equity).
Company buys back 1,000 shares at $30: Dr. Treasury Stock $30,000 / Cr. Cash $30,000. Balance sheet shows this as a deduction from equity.
Mini Q: 10,000 shares issued, 1,500 in treasury. How many shares are outstanding? 8,500 shares (10,000 − 1,500). Outstanding = shares in investors' hands = used for EPS and dividend calculations.
Cash Flow Statement
Indirect Method (CFO)간접법
Starts with Net Income and adjusts for: (1) non-cash items like depreciation and amortization, (2) gains/losses on asset sales, and (3) changes in working capital accounts. Most common format in practice.
NI + Depreciation − A/R increase + Inventory decrease ± other WC changes = CFO
Mini Q: Why is an increase in Accounts Payable ADDED in the CFO section? Liability increased = company owes more to suppliers = kept more cash (didn't pay out). Asset/Cash effect is positive. Liability ↑ → Cash ↑ → Add.
CFO / CFI / CFF영업/투자/재무 활동 현금흐름
Three sections of the cash flow statement: Operating (day-to-day business), Investing (buying/selling long-term assets), Financing (borrowing/repaying debt, issuing/repurchasing stock, paying dividends).
CFO: collect from customers, pay suppliers. CFI: buy equipment, sell investments. CFF: issue bonds, pay dividends.
Mini Q: A company pays $5,000 interest on bonds. Under US GAAP, which section is this classified? CFO — under US GAAP, interest paid is classified as Operating. (Note: under IFRS, interest paid may be classified as CFO or CFF at the company's choice.)
Financial Statement Analysis
Liquidity유동성
A company's ability to meet short-term obligations as they come due. Measured by current ratio, quick ratio, and cash ratio. High liquidity = can pay bills without selling long-term assets.
Current Ratio = CA/CL. Quick Ratio = (CA − Inventory)/CL. Cash Ratio = Cash/CL (most stringent).
Mini Q: CA = $300,000 (includes $80,000 inventory). CL = $150,000. What is the Quick Ratio? Quick Ratio = ($300,000 − $80,000) / $150,000 = $220,000 / $150,000 = 1.47
Earnings Per Share (EPS)주당순이익
Net income attributable to each common share. Required disclosure for public companies. Calculated using weighted average shares outstanding during the period.
NI = $100,000, Preferred Dividends = $10,000, Wtd Avg Shares = 45,000: EPS = ($100,000 − $10,000) / 45,000 = $2.00/share
Mini Q: Why are preferred dividends subtracted from NI before calculating EPS? EPS is for COMMON shareholders only. Preferred dividends are paid first and belong to preferred shareholders — they must be removed to calculate what's left for common stockholders.
Accounts Receivable Turnover매출채권회전율
Measures how efficiently a company collects its credit sales. Higher = faster collection. Days Sales Outstanding (DSO) = 365 / A/R Turnover.
Net Credit Sales = $600,000. Average A/R = $100,000. Turnover = 6. DSO = 365/6 ≈ 61 days to collect.
Mini Q: A/R Turnover drops from 10 to 6 year over year. Is this good or bad? Why? Potentially bad — it means the company is taking longer to collect (from 36 days to 61 days). Could signal credit quality issues or lenient credit terms. Needs investigation.
Debt-to-Equity Ratio부채비율
Total Liabilities / Total Stockholders' Equity. Measures financial leverage — how much of the company is funded by creditors vs. owners. Higher = riskier but potential for higher ROE.
Total Liabilities = $400,000. SE = $200,000. D/E = 2.0 — for every $1 of equity, $2 of debt.
Mini Q: A company with D/E = 3.0 vs. another with D/E = 0.5 — which is riskier? Which might have higher ROE? D/E = 3.0 is riskier (more debt obligations). But leverage can amplify returns — the highly leveraged company may have higher ROE when things go well (but also higher risk of bankruptcy).
Gross Profit / Gross Margin매출총이익 / 매출총이익률
Gross Profit = Net Sales − COGS. Gross Margin = Gross Profit / Net Sales. Shows how much profit remains after direct production costs, before operating expenses.
Sales = $500,000; COGS = $300,000 → Gross Profit = $200,000; Margin = 40%
Mini Q: Gross margin fell from 45% to 38% despite higher sales. What could explain this? Rising COGS (input costs, supply chain issues), competitive pricing pressure forcing price cuts, or a shift in product mix toward lower-margin products.
Return on Assets (ROA)총자산이익률
Net Income / Average Total Assets. Measures how efficiently a company uses ALL its assets to generate profit — regardless of how those assets are financed.
